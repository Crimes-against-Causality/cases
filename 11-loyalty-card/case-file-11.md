# The Loyalty Card

## The crime scene

Ed Simpson climbed the stairs above the tobacconist's on the Strand two days before the mayoral count, carrying a folder he was visibly reluctant to open. He had covered City Hall for the *Ledger* long enough to know the difference between a story and a headline, and this one, Cecily Vane had already decided, was a headline: **HOLT ROUT: LEDGER'S RECORD READER BALLOT PUTS CHALLENGER 24 POINTS CLEAR.** Vane wanted it above the fold in the morning edition. Simpson wanted, first, ten minutes of Wilbert Wright's time.

The mechanics, as Simpson explained them, were straightforward enough. Every household that took the *Ledger*'s home delivery, plus every holder of a Ledger Rewards card—the loyalty scheme the paper had spent a decade building out with petrol stations, department stores, and half the parking garages in the Strand and Aldenmoor—had been mailed a ballot the week before: Crale or Holt, seal it, post it back, postage paid. Two hundred and eleven thousand ballots went out. Forty-seven thousand came back. "Biggest poll this city has ever seen," Simpson said, and to his own evident discomfort, he was quoting Vane's memo. Holt led sixty-two to thirty-eight.

Wright did not reach for the topline number. He reached for the card. "How does one come to hold a Ledger Rewards card?"

"Sign up for home delivery. Or spend enough at a partner shop that they hand you one at the till."

"And how many of Ravenport's registered voters is that?"

Simpson did not have the figure. Neither, it turned out when he rang down to circulation that afternoon, did anyone else at the *Ledger*.

Wright turned to the return numbers next: forty-seven of two hundred and eleven thousand, not quite one in five. He asked what the other four in five had been doing with their ballots. Nobody had thought to ask that either. He closed the folder and slid it back across the desk. "Run it if Cecily insists," he said, "but I would not put money on Councillor Holt, and I would not put money on Mayor Crale. I would want to know a great deal more about the four-fifths of this city that never held a Ledger card in the first place—and the four-fifths of cardholders who held one, and threw the ballot in the bin." Grant the *Ledger* every one of its forty-seven thousand replies, tallied without a single arithmetic error. What, in a poll that size, could still be worth so little?

## The interrogation

1. **True or false?** Forty-seven thousand replies is a larger sample than almost any professional pollster in Harland ever fields for a mayoral race.

2. **Yes or no?** Did every registered voter in Ravenport have a chance of being mailed one of the *Ledger*'s ballots?

3. **Yes or no?** Among the 211,000 people who did receive a ballot, did the *Ledger* have any way of ensuring—or even checking—who bothered to send one back?

4. Two separate decisions shaped who ended up in that stack of 47,000 replies. What were they, and what kind of Ravenport resident did each one favor?

5. **True or false?** A bigger raw number of replies automatically means a more representative sample.

6. What is the fundamental flaw in reading the *Ledger*'s poll as a preview of the result?

## Answers

1. **True.** Most professional polls sample a few hundred to a few thousand voters. Size alone was never the *Ledger*'s problem.

2. **No.** Only subscribers and Rewards cardholders were mailed a ballot—a list built out of delivery routes and retail partnerships, not the electoral roll.

3. **No.** A ballot returned by post is entirely self-selected; nothing compelled anyone to mail it back, and nothing recorded who quietly didn't.

4. First, who got a ballot at all: cardholders cluster in the Strand and Aldenmoor, the city's more affluent, home-owning wards, and are thin on the ground in the outer wards and rental blocks. Second, who bothered to reply: a mailed ballot rewards whoever feels strongly enough to walk it to a postbox, which is rarely an even cross-section of anyone.

5. **False.** A large sample shrinks the *luck* in a number—how much it would wobble on repetition—but it does nothing to fix *who* that number describes. Three times as many badly-drawn respondents just repeats the same mistake more precisely.

6. The poll was never a sample of Ravenport's voters. It was a sample of people who hold a *Ledger* card, filtered again down to the ones who felt like mailing something back—and nothing in that chain points reliably at the other four-fifths of the city.

## What went wrong

The rogue is **[sampling bias](../compendium/sampling-bias.qmd)**, and it enters this case twice, at two different gates, before a single ballot is counted.

The first gate is the mailing list itself. A Ledger Rewards card is not handed out by lottery; it goes to people who already subscribe to a broadsheet's home delivery, or who spend enough at a partner shop to be offered one at the till. That is a specific kind of Ravenport resident—settled, home-owning, disproportionately clustered in the Strand and Aldenmoor—and a poor stand-in for the 640,000 people who are actually entitled to vote. The renters, the residents of the outer wards, anyone who reads the news online rather than on the porch step: none of them appear in the sampling frame at all. No amount of careful tallying afterward puts them back in.

The second gate is the mail itself. A ballot posted to 211,000 people and returned by only 47,000 was never filled in by a random fifth of that list—it was filled in by whichever fifth felt strongly enough to find a stamp. That kind of self-selection tends to reward the most engaged and the most aggrieved, and there is no reason to expect it rewards them evenly across the two campaigns.

Both gates push in directions nobody at the *Ledger* checked, and either alone could have been enough to hand the poll to the wrong candidate. Together, they turned a genuinely enormous mailing into a number that described a fairly narrow slice of the city, twice filtered, and then reported it as a preview of the whole electorate's mind. The size of the sample was never in question and never the point; a poll built this way could have mailed two million ballots and told you no more about how Ravenport would actually vote.

On election night, Mayor Crale was returned with fifty-four percent of the vote to Councillor Holt's forty-six—a comfortable win, delivered by a city rather different from the one that filled out the *Ledger*'s envelopes.

## Background

Ravenport's ballot has a real ancestor: the *Literary Digest*'s 1936 U.S. presidential poll, still the most cited polling failure in the textbooks. The magazine mailed some ten million straw ballots drawn chiefly from its own subscriber list, telephone directories, and automobile registrations, got back about 2.4 million, and forecast a comfortable win for Alf Landon over Franklin Roosevelt. Roosevelt won in a landslide, carrying 46 of 48 states. Telephones and cars were, in the depths of the Depression, disproportionately owned by wealthier, more Republican-leaning households—and, as later reanalysis showed, Landon supporters were also simply more likely to mail their ballots back. Meanwhile George Gallup, polling a far smaller but deliberately representative sample of a few thousand voters, called the race correctly. The *Digest* folded within two years.

The lesson has only sharpened in the age of enormous, easily gathered data. As the statistician Xiao-Li Meng has argued, a poorly-drawn sample does not improve as it grows—it can, in a precise statistical sense, become more confidently wrong, because a bigger biased sample shrinks the error bars around the wrong answer rather than correcting it. A hundred million self-selected survey responses can carry less real information about a population than a few thousand properly drawn ones. Scale answers the question "how much noise is left?" It has no opinion on the separate question of who was asked in the first place.
