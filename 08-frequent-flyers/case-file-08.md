# The Frequent Flyers

## The crime scene

The healthcare system in Ravenport has never been as overburdened. Waiting times in emergency departments routinely exceed five hours, and staffing shortages are so severe that even the most urgent cases cannot always be prioritized. As a result, an estimated 2% of patients die before ever being seen by a physician.

One of the key drivers of this crisis is a small group of 'frequent flyers' – patients who account for a disproportionate share of hospital use and costs, as they typically suffer from multiple chronic conditions and very regularly end up in hospital. In Ravenport, the top 5% of patients account for 50% of total hospital expenditures, while the top 1% alone account for nearly 25%. Even modest reductions in hospital visits among this group could substantially relieve pressure on the system.

In response, Ravenport's Ministry of Health launched a care coordination intervention. The target patients of this intervention had at least two hospitalizations in the previous six months on their electronic health record. 800 eligible patients were enrolled into this structured care-coordination intervention during an inpatient stay, with the primary objective of reducing hospital readmissions within 180 days of discharge.

The intervention developed personalised care plans addressing both medical needs and social determinants of health. Multidisciplinary teams coordinated outpatient follow-up, facilitated access to social services, and conducted post-discharge outreach via phone calls and home visits in an effort to reduce the fragmentation of their care between different care providers.

The results of the pilot programme were striking – hospital admissions among the frequent flyers in the intervention group fell by 40% in the six months following discharge! At last, a long-awaited breakthrough in Ravenport’s healthcare system… but Wilbert Wright was not convinced. Why?<br><br>


## The interrogation

1. What characterises the frequent flyers?

2. Yes or no? Was there a comparison group of similarly high-use patients who did not receive the intervention?

3. How much is a 40% reduction in absolute terms?

4. Did all enrolled patients engage or benefit equally?

5. What do we not know? 

6. What factors other than the intervention could explain the reduction in hospital admissions?

7. What can and can’t we conclude?<br><br>


## Answers

1. The frequent flyer patients comprise a small minority of patients who often suffer from multiple chronic diseases and use hospital services much more than the average individual. Despite being a small minority they however account for a disproportionately large share of healthcare expenditure.

2. There might have been, but from the description we don't know for sure - it just reports that 800 eligible patients were included. Importantly, however, the reported results are not based on the comparison with an untreated group.

3. We cannot determine this from the information provided. To assess how much a relative decrease by 40% is in absolute terms, we would need to know how many hospitalisations occurred before or after the intervention. From the description, we know that they had "at least two hospitalizations in the previous six months", but that information is not sufficient to calculate an absolute number.

4. We cannot infer this from the information provided. We only know how many patients were enrolled, but not the degree to which each patient engaged with different elements of the programme. Furthermore, the reported 40% reduction represents an average across all enrolled patients. It is possible that there is substantial variation between patients but it could also be a similar change for all enrolled patients. 

5. We lack some important information to assess the effectiveness of the programme, including whether there was a control or comparison group; whether patients were randomly assigned to receive the intervention; the number of hospitalisations before and after the programme; how many patients fully engaged with the care coordination programme; whether the patients' characteristics changed over time; whether other changes in healthcare initiatives happened simultaneously; and whether the reduction was sustained beyond the six-month follow-up period.

6. Several alternative explanations are possible: The reduction in hospitalisations could be driven by regression to the mean, where patients who were identified because they were hospitalised unusually frequently return to a more common frequency over time. Furthermore, the disease severity could fluctuate, with some patients recovering irrespective of the intervention. We can also not rule any other changes to the hospital admission system or changes in other parts of the healthcare system that could have produced the reduction observed. Without a comparison group, it is difficult to determine how much of the observed reduction is a result of the intervention itself.

7. We can conclude that hospitalisations among the enrolled patients were 40% lower during the six months after discharge than during the six months before the intervention. However, we cannot conclude that the care coordination programme caused this reduction. Because the study compares patients before and after the intervention without a control group, alternative explanations cannot be ruled out. Stronger study designs, such as a randomized controlled trial or a well-designed quasi-experiment with an appropriate comparison group, would be needed to infer the presence or absence of a causal effect.<br><br>


## What went wrong

The conclusion about the effectiveness of the care coordination programme was premature. Without a control group, we cannot know whether the reduction in hospitalisations was caused by the intervention and/or by other factors.

In addition, patients were not enrolled at random. They were enrolled precisely because they had just been through an unusually bad stretch, defined by the eligibility rule itself: at least two hospitalizations in the prior six months. Any group singled out for an extreme run, whether it's the sickest patients, the worst-performing schools, or the most accident-prone junctions, tends to look less extreme the next time you measure it. Some of that "improvement" might be a consequence of the intervention, some of it is simply that an unusually bad spell was never going to repeat itself indefinitely. This phenomenon is called "regression to the mean".

A wisely constructed control group could have addressed this. For instance, enroll the eligible patients, then randomly assign half to the care-coordination programme and half to usual care. Both groups were selected the same way, on the same extreme criterion, so both should regress toward the mean by roughly the same amount — any difference between them that remains is a fair candidate for the programme's actual effect. Without that comparison, a before-and-after number cannot separate "the programme worked" from "this group was always going to look better next time" — the two are observationally identical.

The pilot's 40% reduction is therefore consistent with the programme doing nothing at all. It is also consistent with the programme doing a great deal. The uncomfortable truth is that the number in hand cannot tell the two apart, and no amount of scrutinizing the same before-and-after figures more closely will fix that — the fix has to happen in the design, before the data are collected, not after.

## Background

The Camden Coalition in the United States set up a coordinated care programme for patients in New Jersey who were in and out of hospital very frequently, aiming to stabilise them through intensive and tailored support and shift the disproportionate burden from hospitals to primary care providers and specialists. To test the effectiveness of this measure, they conducted a randomised controlled trial. The main result was disappointing, as the intervention did not reduce readmissions overall compared to the control group, observing a similar trend in both groups. Nonetheless, the study found that patients enrolled in the intervention ended up making better use of outpatient primary and specialist care, and that those who engaged more with the programme were less likely to be readmitted, and thus the study is a good example that interventions may improve other parts of healthcare even when they do not achieve the main objective specified.<br><br>
