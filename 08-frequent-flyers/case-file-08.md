# The Frequent Flyers

## The crime scene

The healthcare system in Ravenport has never been as overburdened. Waiting times in emergency departments routinely exceed five hours, and staffing shortages are so severe that even the most urgent cases cannot always be prioritized. As a result, an estimated 2% of patients die before ever being seen by a physician.

One of the key drivers of this crisis is a small group of 'frequent flyers' – patients who account for a disproportionate share of hospital use and costs, as they typically suffer from multiple chronic conditions and very regularly end up in hospital. In Ravenport, the top 5% of patients account for 50% of total hospital expenditures, while the top 1% alone account for nearly 25%. Even modest reductions in hospital visits among this group could substantially relieve pressure on the system.

In response, Ravenport's Ministry of Health launched a care coordination intervention. The target patients of this intervention had at least two hospitalizations in the previous six months on their electronic health record. 800 eligible patients were enrolled into this structured care-coordination intervention during an inpatient stay, with the primary objective of reducing hospital readmissions within 180 days of discharge.

The intervention developed personalised care plans addressing both medical needs and social determinants of health. Multidisciplinary teams coordinated outpatient follow-up, facilitated access to social services, and conducted post-discharge outreach via phone calls and home visits in an effort to reduce the fragmentation of their care between different care providers.

The results of the pilot programme were striking – hospital admissions among the frequent flyers fell by 40% in the six months following discharge! At last, a long-awaited breakthrough in Ravenport’s healthcare system… but Wilbert Wright was not convinced. Why?<br><br>


## The interrogation

1. What characterises the frequent flyers?

2. Who was eligible for the care coordination programme? 

3. What exactly did the care coordination programme deliver?

4. How much is a 40% reduction in absolute terms?

5. Could this reduction have occurred without the intervention? 

6. Did all enrolled patients engage equally?

7. Did all enrolled patients benefit equally?

8. What other factors could explain the change?

9. What do we not know? 

10. What can and can’t we conclude?<br><br>


## Answers

1. The frequent flyer patients comprise a small minority of patients who often suffer from multiple chronic diseases and use hospital services much more than the average individual. Despite being a small minority they however account for a disproportionately large share of healthcare expenditure.

2. Patients who had at least two hospitalisations to their name in the past six months were eligible.

3. The care coordination programme provided targeted care plans accounting for the patient's respective medical and social needs and organised follow-up appointments. The goal was to prevent unnecessary readmissions and ensuring that the patients receive continuous care.

4. We cannot determine this from the information provided. To assess how much a relative decrease by 40% is in absolute terms we would need to know how many hospitalisations occurred before or after the intervention.

5. Yes. For example, patients may naturally be hospitalised less frequently after a period of unusually many hospitalisations, a phenomenon known as *regression to the mean*. Other changes happening simultaneously, such as improvements in healthcare services or seasonal patterns in illness, could also contribute to the observed reduction.

6. We cannot infer this from the information provided. We only know how many patients were enrolled, but not the degree to which each patient engaged with different elements of the programme.

7. We also cannot infer this from the information provided. The reported 40% reduction represents an average across all enrolled patients. It is possible that there is substantial variation between patients but it could also be a similar change for all enrolled patients. 

8. Several alternative explanations are possible: The reduction in hospitalisations could be driven by regression to the mean, where patients who were identified because they were hospitalised unusually frequently return to a more common frequency over time. Furthermore, the disease severity could fluctuate, with some patients recovering irrespective of the intervention. We can also not rule any other changes to the hospital admission system or changes in other parts of the healthcare system that could have produced the reduction observed. Without a comparison group, it is difficult to determine how much of the observed reduction is a result of the intervention itself.

9. We lack some important information to assess the effectiveness of the programme, including whether there was a control or comparison group; whether patients were randomly assigned to receive the intervention; the number of hospitalisations before and after the programme; how many patients fully engaged with the care coordination programme; whether the patients' characteristics changed over time; whether other changes in healthcare initiatives happened simultaneously; and whether the reduction was sustained beyond the six-month follow-up period.

10. We can conclude that hospitalisations among the enrolled patients were 40% lower during the six months after discharge than during the six months before the intervention. However, we cannot conclude that the care coordination programme caused this reduction. Because the study compares patients before and after the intervention without a control group, alternative explanations cannot be ruled out. Stronger study designs, such as a randomized controlled trial or a well-designed quasi-experiment with an appropriate comparison group, would be needed to infer the presence or absence of a causal effect.<br><br>


## What went wrong

The conclusion about the effectiveness of the care coordination programme was premature. Without a control group, we cannot know whether the reduction in hospitalisations was caused by the intervention and/or by other factors.<br><br>


## Background

The Camden Coalition in the United States set up a coordinated care programme for patients in New Jersey who were in and out of hospital very frequently, aiming to stabilise them through intensive and tailored support and shift the disproportionate burden from hospitals to primary care providers and specialists. To test the effectiveness of this measure, they conducted a randomised controlled trial. The main result was disappointing, as the intervention did not reduce readmissions overall compared to the control group, observing a similar trend in both groups. Nonetheless, the study found that patients enrolled in the intervention ended up making better use of outpatient primary and specialist care, and that those who engaged more with the programme were less likely to be readmitted, and thus the study is a good example that interventions may improve other parts of healthcare even when they do not achieve the main objective specified.<br><br>
