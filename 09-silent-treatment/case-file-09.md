# The Silent Treatment

## The crime scene

Wilbert Wright still keeps one afternoon a month for Bram Ostler, whom he has known since before either of them had grey in his hair. Bram gardens the way other men follow football—completely, and with strong opinions—and this month he had news. He led Wilbert through the kitchen to the little lean-to greenhouse at the back, wiped his hands on his apron, and presented, with the shy pride of a man unveiling a portrait, two coleus plants on the potting bench.

"Same soil, same water, same light, the lot," Bram said. "I was scrupulous. The only difference"—he tapped the left-hand pot—"is that this one spent a fortnight being shouted at." He had rigged a small speaker over the plant and run it at what he cheerfully called *subway volume*—a hundred decibels, near enough—morning to night for two weeks. The other plant sat a few feet away in peace. "And look."

Wilbert looked. The shouted-at coleus had wilted; its neighbour was glossy and upright. "There," said Bram, folding his arms. "Plants don't like to be yelled at. It's not their feelings—it's the racket. Sets them giving off more water, and they flag. I flipped a coin over which pot got the speaker, before you ask, so it's all above board."

Somewhere behind Wilbert's polite expression, a small professional part of him laughed until it wept. But Bram was beaming, and Bram was his oldest friend, and there are things one does not do to an oldest friend in his own greenhouse. Wilbert said the plants looked very well cared for—which was true—and kept the rest to himself. He was in no position to sneer, in any case: on his own desk at home sat a coleus he greeted every morning and consulted, quietly, on the harder problems.

So he said nothing. But he could not stop thinking it. Grant Bram everything he claims—identical care, an honest coin flip to decide which plant faced the noise—and there are *still* one or two ordinary explanations for the wilt that owe nothing to sound. What are they?

## The interrogation

1. **Yes or no?** Did both plants have the same chance of being treated with noise?

2. **Yes or no?** As he tended and judged the two plants day to day, did Bram know which one was the treated plant?

3. **Yes or no?** Could one of the two plants have wilted within a fortnight even if neither had heard a sound?

4. Besides the noise, what rival explanations could account for the wilt?

5. How would you run Bram's experiment so that a wilt really could be blamed on the noise?

## Answers

1. **Yes.** If Bram used a fair coin (and we are given no reason to not trust him), the noisy pot was chosen at random. That is the kind of safeguard a good experiment uses to avoid stacking the deck.

2. **Yes.** He set both pots up and tended them himself, knowing all along which one was on trial. Nothing was hidden from the person doing the watering—and the judging.

3. **Yes.** Plants differ. Pot up two look-alike seedlings under identical care and one may flourish while the other fails—every gardener has watched it happen.

4. We can think of two, at least. First, plain coincidence: two plants are never identical, and the frailer one—by luck of the coin—drew the noise; it might have wilted in silence just the same. Second, Bram himself: knowing which plant was "meant" to suffer, he could, without ever intending to, have watered it a little less or handled it more roughly. Either could wilt a plant with no help from the sound.

5. Grow *many* plants per group and split them at random, so the groups start alike on average. Let someone who does not know which plants are treated do the watering and the judging, so expectations cannot leak in. And keep the noisy and quiet plants in separate rooms, so the racket meant for one group cannot spill onto the other.

## What went wrong

Bram's design has two soft spots—and, worth saying, neither is a hanging offence on its own.

The first is the [**small sample** (see the Rogues' Gallery)](../compendium/small-samples.qmd). With a single plant in each group, the coin flip that felt so fair in fact settles very little: it cannot make two living plants equally hardy; it only decides which one meets the speaker. If the pair differed in vigour—and two seedlings always do—the wilt could be the noise, or could simply be that the frailer plant, by the luck of the toss, was the one put on trial.

This is not to say two plants can never teach you anything—small samples are not automatically worthless. What is thin here is the *evidence*, not merely the count. Had Bram watched both plants thrive for months and then, days after the speaker came on, seen one collapse, that sudden turn lining up with the treatment would be far harder to dismiss; a long, steady record broken at the moment of the intervention carries real weight. But what he actually has is two plants and a single snapshot at the end of a fortnight—probably not enough to draw the conclusions he drew.

The second soft spot is that the test was not **blind**. Bram knew exactly which plant was meant to suffer, and expectation is a quiet thing: a gardener who "knows" the noisy plant should flag may water it a shade less, handle it more roughly, or simply notice its droop a beat sooner—none of it deliberate, all of it enough to tip a close call. That is what researchers call the ["observer-expectancy effect" (see the Rogues' Gallery)](../compendium/observer-expectancy.qmd). It is why careful experiments hide the assignment from whoever tends and judges the subjects, so the hoped-for result cannot, even honestly, shape itself.

And behind both sits a rule worth keeping: when a claimed effect has no plausible mechanism—when it is genuinely unclear *how* shouting would wilt a plant—hold your confidence loosely, and ask for more before you believe. A dramatic result resting on a shaky story is exactly the kind that later dissolves into coincidence. Which is not to say the underlying question is silly.

## Background

The instinct behind Bram's greenhouse is not as eccentric as it first sounds. Careful modern research has shown that plants genuinely sense—and even make—sound. A team led by Lilach Hadany in Tel Aviv found that evening-primrose flowers, acting as ears, sweeten their nectar within *minutes* of "hearing" the low-frequency hum of a passing bee, the petals themselves vibrating to the pollinator's wingbeats (Veits et al., 2019). Heidi Appel and Reginald Cocroft showed that a plant played back the vibrations of a caterpillar chewing a leaf pre-emptively ramps up its chemical defences—and can tell that chewing apart from wind or insect song (Appel & Cocroft, 2014). And in 2023 the Tel Aviv group reported the converse: stressed plants *emit* airborne ultrasonic clicks—dozens an hour when thirsty or cut—that a machine can learn to read, distinguishing a dehydrated tomato from a healthy one (Khait et al., 2023).

So plants "hearing," and even "speaking," is real science. But notice what those studies share and Bram's lacks: many plants, silent controls, blinded playback, and effects that hold up across replicate after replicate. The findings survive because the *designs* let them. A single coleus, however loudly scolded, can settle nothing—which is the whole difference between a good story and evidence. As for whether merely shouting at a plant wilts it: that, fittingly, has never been properly shown.
